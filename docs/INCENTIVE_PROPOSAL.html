Fair Incentives for Federated Mental-Health Learning

A Shapley-Driven, Noise-Robust Incentive Mechanism Extending ShapFed, with a Tabular Depression-Screening Case Study

Project: FedPowerShap → FedShapIn (working title)  |  Advisor: Prof. Lekha  |  Date: July 2026  |  Revision 2

Document type: Research proposal (pre-implementation)  |  Setting: Cross-silo federated learning on tabular mental-health survey data

Revision 2 addresses the review comment “but this methodology we failed in our health dataset, right?” — see §5, which states the prior negative result in full, and §10, which pre-registers falsifiable success criteria.

Abstract. Federated learning (FL) lets multiple institutions train a shared model without pooling raw data — a natural fit for privacy-sensitive mental-health screening, where clinics, universities, and health apps hold small, heterogeneous, and noisily-labelled cohorts. But FL is only sustainable if participants are fairly rewarded for genuine contribution; otherwise high-quality data holders drop out and free-riders dilute the model. The state-of-the-art contribution estimator, ShapFed (Tastan et al., IJCAI 2024), computes class-specific Shapley values and uses them for weighted aggregation, but it stops at measurement: it defines no reward rule and proves no economic property (individual rationality, incentive-compatibility, budget balance). We propose FedShapIn, an incentive layer that converts a noise-robust contribution signal into rewards with stated economic properties, evaluated on a public tabular depression-screening benchmark partitioned across simulated institutions with injected free-riders.

We state the central risk to this proposal up front. Our own prior study (§5) tested contribution-aware aggregation on three medical-imaging datasets and found that under 40% label noise it did not beat the strongest baseline (FedDyn) on any of them — a tie on OCTMNIST and losses on ISIC and APTOS. That is a real negative result, and this proposal does not hide behind it. What the same study did establish is that the underlying quality signal correctly ranks clients: corrupted clients received 0.69× the aggregation weight of clean clients, reliably and from round 1. FedShapIn depends on the ranking property, not on the accuracy property that failed. §5 argues this distinction precisely, §10 converts it into pre-registered, falsifiable success criteria that do not include beating FedDyn on accuracy, and §10.3 commits us to reporting the aggregation comparison on the new dataset even if it fails again. No results are reported here — this is a proposal.

1. Introduction and motivation

Mental-health conditions such as depression and anxiety are widespread and under-diagnosed, and machine learning on screening data (questionnaires, lifestyle, and wearable-derived features) is a promising triage aid. The data, however, is siloed and sensitive: a single university counselling centre, a clinic, or a wellness-app operator each holds only a few hundred to a few thousand records, and privacy regulation plus stigma make centralised pooling impractical. Federated learning (FL) is the standard answer: each site trains locally and shares only model updates. A dedicated survey of FL for mental health in education [11] documents exactly this setting — high-school and college cohorts held by institutions that cannot legally pool them.

Two properties of the mental-health setting make plain FL insufficient:

  1. Participation is voluntary and costly. Institutions spend compute, curation effort, and accept privacy risk to join. Without a credible, fair reward for genuine contribution, the best data holders have no reason to stay, and free-riders — sites that submit low-effort or fabricated updates to harvest the shared model — are not deterred. This is the classic FL incentive problem [2,3].
  2. Contribution is heterogeneous and hard to measure honestly. Cohorts differ in size, class balance, and label reliability (self-report scales are noisy). A reward mechanism must therefore rest on a contribution estimate that is robust to noise and to strategic manipulation, not merely a proxy such as dataset size or self-declared quality.
Thesis. The FedPowerShap project has a verified per-client quality signal (§5.2) but could not convert it into an accuracy win over FedDyn (§5.1). The natural next step is therefore not another attempt at the accuracy claim. It is to apply the signal to the problem it actually solves — allocating reward in proportion to contribution — where correct ranking is the requirement and accuracy parity is sufficient.

This reframing is the core intellectual move of the proposal, and it is a narrowing of the claim, not a widening. It is deliberately falsifiable (§10).

2. Background

Federated learning. A server coordinates K clients over communication rounds. Each round, a subset of clients trains on local data and returns updates; the server aggregates them (classically FedAvg, a size-weighted average). Data is typically non-IID across clients, which degrades naive averaging and motivates robust aggregators (FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedDyn, MOON, FedBN).

Shapley value for contribution. From cooperative game theory, the Shapley value fairly divides the "value" of a coalition among its members by averaging each member's marginal contribution over all orderings. In FL it is the principled way to answer "how much did client k help?", and it uniquely satisfies efficiency, symmetry, null-player, and additivity. Its cost (exponential in K) drives approximations — Monte-Carlo permutation sampling, gradient/last-layer proxies (as in ShapFed [1]), and recent accelerations: FedOwen uses Owen sampling to cut estimation cost at fixed budget [8], and DPVS-Shapley targets the same bottleneck [9].

Incentive-mechanism properties. A reward/payment mechanism is judged on well-defined economic criteria, which this proposal adopts as its evaluation axes:

  • Individual Rationality (IR): every honest participant's utility (reward minus cost) is non-negative — nobody is worse off for joining.
  • Incentive Compatibility (IC) / truthfulness: a participant's best strategy is to behave honestly; manipulation does not pay.
  • Budget Balance (BB): total rewards stay within a fixed budget (weak BB) or exactly exhaust it (strong BB).
  • Fairness: reward is monotone in — and ideally proportional to — true contribution; measurable via the reward–contribution correlation and Jain's index on payoffs.
  • Free-rider / Byzantine resistance: clients contributing little or adversarial updates receive little or no reward and are down-weighted in aggregation.

3. Related work (literature survey)

(a) Shapley-based contribution in FL. ShapFed [1] computes Class-Specific Shapley Values (CSSV) from last-classifier-layer gradients and aggregates via contribution-weighted averaging (ShapFed-WA), improving class-imbalanced performance and enabling contribution-proportional personalization. A large-scale experimental evaluation of contribution estimators [10] benchmarks this family systematically.

(b) The fragility of single-round gradient signals — external corroboration of our negative result. Two recent papers independently identify the failure mode we measured (§5.2). CoAst [12] states that existing validation-free methods "assess the contribution based on the parameters and gradients of local models and the global model in a single training round, which is easily compromised by the stochasticity of model training," and fixes it with cross-round valuation. SpectralFed / SpectralFuse [13] motivates a data-free spectral-entropy signal precisely because existing signals are "susceptible to manipulation." Our anti-discriminative CSSV finding is therefore not an implementation artefact — it is a known-and-independently-reported weakness of cosine-of-update scoring, which strengthens rather than weakens the case for the blended, temporally-smoothed signal in §7.1.

(c) FL incentive mechanisms. Surveys [2,3] group mechanisms into game theory (Stackelberg, Nash), auction theory (reverse and budget-constrained auctions), and contract/reputation theory. Representative systems: FIFL [5] (fair, attack-aware rewards), FedFAIM [6] (model-performance-based fair incentive), Shapley-UCB for data-market seller selection [7], and reward-timing work [14]. FedEach [4] is notable for computing contributions and selecting reliable clients without any server-side validation set, tolerating a high fraction of malicious clients — directly relevant to our robustness goal.

Closest competitor — stated honestly. FRIMFL [15] already combines a reverse auction, a reputation mechanism, and Shapley-based contribution evaluation to reward reliable clients under a budget. Our mechanism is therefore not the first to join Shapley values, reputation, and budgeted reward. Three differences remain, and the proposal must stand on these and not on a false novelty claim:
  • Class-specific rather than scalar Shapley contribution (CSSV), which matters when sites differ in prevalence rather than in size.
  • A measured, published failure analysis of the contribution signal under partial participation (§5.2) and a signal designed against that specific failure — where FRIMFL assumes its Shapley signal is reliable, we have evidence that the naive version is not.
  • An explicit adversary model with graded free-rider strength (§9.3) and pre-registered falsification thresholds (§10), rather than a demonstration on a favourable setting.

(d) FL for mental health. The CMES survey [16] documents the privacy motivation and data-silo problem for computational mental healthcare; the education-focused survey [11] covers the student cohort specifically. Applied work includes FedMental [17] (depression detection from social-media text, which reports FL within ~2.5 F1 of centralized but a severe differential-privacy trade-off), on-device FL for depression detection from Reddit posts [18], and privacy-preserving EEG-based MDD screening on MODMA [19]. None of these define or evaluate an incentive mechanism.

Gap in the literature. Shapley-based FL work measures contribution but mostly stops short of a reward rule with economic guarantees; FL incentive work that does close the loop (FRIMFL, FIFL, FedFAIM) uses coarse or assumed-reliable contribution signals and is not validated under the partial-participation failure mode of §5.2; and FL-for-mental-health work uses no incentive mechanism at all. FedShapIn targets that intersection — a noise-robust, class-specific contribution signal driving a budget-balanced reward rule, instantiated on mental-health data.

4. Base paper deep-dive: ShapFed

What it does. ShapFed treats the rows of each client's last-layer weight update as class-specific gradient signals. The server computes, per client k and class c, the cosine similarity between that client's class-c update and a reference (consensus) update — the Class-Specific Shapley Value CSSVk,c. Aggregated per client, these become normalized aggregation weights (ShapFed-WA), and can be used to hand each client a personalized model scaled to its contribution. Reported wins are strongest under class imbalance, where size-weighted FedAvg under-serves minority classes.

Why it is the right base paper. It is already the intellectual anchor of FedPowerShap; extending it keeps continuity with the group's codebase and prior report, and its per-class contribution scores are exactly the substrate an incentive layer needs.

Critique / limitations we target.
  • No incentive mechanism. ShapFed converts contribution into aggregation weights and personalized models, never into an explicit reward/payment, and proves no economic property (IR, IC, budget balance). Contribution measurement ≠ incentive mechanism.
  • Fragile signal under realistic conditions. Our measurements (§5.2) found the CSSV cosine signal anti-discriminative under partial participation and label noise: corrupted clients receive higher scores. A reward built on raw CSSV is therefore manipulable. Independent work [12,13] reports the same class of weakness.
  • No mental-health / tabular validation. ShapFed is evaluated on generic image benchmarks; tiny-cohort, self-report-noisy mental-health data is untested.

5. Prior results, including what failed (scope of the negative result)

This section exists because it is the first question any reviewer — or advisor — will ask. It reports the prior study's outcome without softening, then delimits exactly what it does and does not imply.

Prior protocol. Three medical-imaging FL benchmarks (OCTMNIST retinal OCT, ISIC dermoscopy, APTOS-2019 diabetic retinopathy), Dirichlet α=0.3 non-IID, ResNet-18 @ 64px, 10 clients with 5 active per round, 2 local epochs, SGD lr=1e-3 cosine-decayed, 40 rounds, 20k-sample train cap, last-round metric, 3–5 seeds. Noise model: a fraction ρ of clients have 60% of training labels flipped; test labels stay clean. Frozen in scripts/run_noise_sweep.py; full tables in results/crossdataset_tables.md.

5.1 What failed: the accuracy claim under noise

Dataset (40% noisy clients) FedAvg FedDyn Proposed Δ vs FedDyn
OCTMNIST 0.462 0.523 0.518 −0.006 (tie)
ISIC 0.669 0.672 0.645 −0.027 (loss)
APTOS 0.577 0.649 0.588 −0.061 (loss)

FedDyn is at least as good on every dataset. Fairness (Jain) and worst-client accuracy at 40% also favour FedDyn (e.g. APTOS worst-client 0.152 vs 0.048). On clean data the proposed method reaches parity (OCTMNIST 0.708 vs 0.688, n=5; APTOS 0.728 vs 0.715; ISIC 0.719 vs 0.734) — all within seed variance.

The diagnosis was itself a finding: define the "opening" as how much 40% noise costs FedDyn. It is large only on OCTMNIST (0.158) and small on APTOS (0.066) and ISIC (0.062). FedDyn's dynamic regularization already absorbs moderate label noise, so down-weighting mostly discards usable data and loses. Conclusion recorded in the prior report: there is no general robustness win from contribution-aware aggregation in this regime. We do not re-litigate that here.

5.2 What worked: the ranking claim

Two results survive, and both are about identifying clients rather than about accuracy.

  • The loss-based detector ranks correctly. On OCTMNIST at 40% noise (3 seeds), corrupted clients received 0.156 mean aggregation weight versus 0.228 for clean clients — a 0.69 ratio, stable from round 1 onward (figures/paper/mechanism_weights_n40.png). The signal is w ∝ exp(−β·(loss_k − min_loss)) where loss_k is the current global model's loss on client k's own data (federated/server.py::_loss_weights).
  • A reportable negative result about CSSV. Scoring clients by cosine similarity of the classifier update against the consensus is anti-discriminative here: corrupted clients score higher (noisy/clean weight ratio ≈ 1.1–1.3), even with a coordinate-wise-median reference. Cause: with 5 of 10 clients active and 40% corrupted, the active set typically contains 2–3 corrupted clients, so the consensus direction is itself pulled toward the corrupted updates and the robust-reference assumption breaks. This is why the method switched to the loss detector, and why §7.1 does not use raw CSSV.

5.3 Why the incentive claim is a different claim

The two claims are logically independent.
  • Failed claim (§5.1): re-weighting clients by estimated quality yields a more accurate global model than FedDyn. This requires that suppressing a noisy client's data helps more than losing that data hurts — a net-benefit condition that depends on the baseline's own noise tolerance, and which does not hold when the baseline already absorbs the noise.
  • Proposed claim (this document): the estimated quality ranks clients correctly, so a reward proportional to it pays honest contributors and starves free-riders. This requires only monotonicity of the score in true contribution. §5.2 is direct evidence for it, measured on the same health data where the accuracy claim failed.
A mechanism can allocate reward correctly while the same weights fail to improve accuracy — because reward allocation is a ranking/normalization problem, whereas accuracy is a bias–variance problem in which discarding noisy-but-informative gradients has a real cost.

Two further reasons the new setting is more favourable than the failed one — both testable, not assumed:

  1. Free-riders carry no signal; noisy labels do. A 60%-label-flip client still produces partially informative gradients, which is precisely why suppressing it cost accuracy in §5.1. A free-rider submitting a near-zero or randomised update contributes no usable information, so denying it weight and reward has no offsetting cost. The adversary this proposal targets is strictly easier to exclude.
  2. The metric no longer needs a margin. §5.1 needed to beat a strong baseline on accuracy. §10 asks only for non-inferiority on accuracy, and moves the burden of proof onto incentive metrics for which no competitive baseline exists in this domain.
Residual risk we accept. If the score's monotonicity also degrades on tabular data, FedShapIn fails — the §5.2 evidence is from one modality (medical images) and one corruption type (label flips). §10.2 makes this the primary falsification test and §10.3 commits to publishing the outcome either way.

7. Problem statement and research questions

We study cross-silo FL for binary depression screening across K institutions, a fraction of which are unreliable (label-noisy or free-riding). The server holds a fixed per-round reward budget B and must both (a) train an accurate, fair global model and (b) allocate rewards that reflect true contribution.

  • RQ1 (fidelity — primary): Does FedShapIn's reward correlate with each client's true contribution (leave-one-out held-out marginal value) more faithfully than size-proportional, equal-split, and raw-CSSV rewards?
  • RQ2 (robustness / IC — primary): Under graded free-riders and label noise, does FedShapIn deny reward to and down-weight bad actors — i.e. is honesty the best response across the attack ladder?
  • RQ3 (non-inferiority — secondary): Is FedShapIn's screening performance statistically non-inferior to FedDyn, and how does it trade fairness (Jain on accuracy and on payoff) against the nine baselines? Note: this is deliberately posed as non-inferiority, not superiority — see §5.1.
  • RQ4 (scope of the prior negative result): Does the §5.1 finding (no aggregation win under noise) replicate on tabular mental-health data? A negative answer would extend the prior characterization to a new modality; a positive answer would bound it to imaging. Either is reportable.

8. Proposed method: FedShapIn

Overview. FedShapIn adds an incentive layer on top of ShapFed's contribution substrate, in three parts.

8.1 Noise-robust contribution signal. Instead of raw CSSV, define a per-client per-round score

s_k = α·CSSV_k + β·q_k(loss) + γ·rep_k ,   α+β+γ=1

where qk(loss) is the loss-based quality weight — the §5.2 fix for CSSV's anti-discriminative failure — and repk is the reputation EMA, which rewards consistent contribution and damps single-round manipulation. The EMA term is also the mechanism CoAst [12] identifies as the cure for single-round instability, so its inclusion is theoretically motivated and not merely smoothing. All three components already exist in the codebase (§6). The (α,β,γ) weights are ablated (§9.4); given §5.2 we expect β to dominate and α may be driven to 0 — an outcome we will report plainly, since "the class-specific Shapley term contributes nothing" is itself a finding about ShapFed.

8.2 Reward rule (budget-balanced Shapley payoff). Given budget B per round and non-negative scores sk over active clients, allocate

r_k = B · s_k / Σ_j s_j

as the primary rule (strong budget balance by construction; fairness since reward is monotone in contribution). Two comparison designs: a reputation-gated variant zeroing reward below a reputation floor (free-rider deterrence), and a reverse-auction variant in the style of FRIMFL [15]. Rewards accumulate across rounds into a per-client payoff used for settlement and for contribution-proportional personalization (inherited from ShapFed).

8.3 Claimed properties (to be argued formally + shown empirically).

  • Budget balance: exact by the normalization in 8.2.
  • Individual rationality: honest clients receive reward monotone in genuine contribution; with a cost model, IR holds above a minimum contribution threshold (to be characterised).
  • Approximate incentive-compatibility: because the score blends a manipulation-resistant loss signal with a slow reputation term, single-round gaming (gradient scaling, label flipping, update replay) lowers rather than raises reward. The empirical test is RQ2. We scope the claim to the attack classes in §9.3 and do not claim IC in general.
  • Fairness: high reward–contribution correlation and high Jain payoff index (RQ1, RQ3).

9. Dataset and experimental design

9.1 Dataset (facts verified, July 2026)

Modality choice. A public tabular mental-health survey dataset: it plugs into the existing harness (no image/EEG/audio pipeline), needs no data-use agreement (unlike DAIC-WOZ), and is large enough to partition across simulated institutions (unlike the ~55-subject MODMA EEG set).

Primary — Student Depression Dataset (Kaggle, adilshamim8 / hopesb mirrors) [20]: 27,901 records, reducing to ~27,826 after dropping missing values. Features: Gender, Age, City, Profession, Academic Pressure, Work Pressure, CGPA, Study Satisfaction, Job Satisfaction, Sleep Duration, Dietary Habits, Degree, Have you ever had suicidal thoughts?, Work/Study Hours, Financial Stress, Family History of Mental Illness. Target: binary Depression.

Two dataset corrections from revision 1, both of which change the experiment design:
  • The target is majority-positive, not a minority class. Mean of Depression is 0.586 — 58.6% positive, 41.4% negative. Revision 1 described a "realistic minority class"; that was wrong. Two consequences: (i) 58.6% is far above clinical prevalence (~15–35%), so this is a self-selected survey sample and we will say so rather than calling it epidemiological; (ii) ShapFed's reported advantage is strongest under class imbalance, which a near-balanced global target does not provide. The imbalance must therefore come from the federated partition — Dirichlet α=0.3 over the label gives per-site prevalence skew, which is the realistic mechanism anyway (clinics and counselling centres differ in case mix). We will report per-site prevalence explicitly.
  • No PHQ-9 scores exist in this dataset. Revision 1 said labels "follow the standard PHQ-9 / GAD-7 ≥ 10 threshold." The Kaggle set ships a pre-computed binary label with no item-level questionnaire scores, so that claim is removed. For a genuinely PHQ-9-scored cross-check we add the PHQ-9 Student Depression Dataset (Mendeley Data, kkzjk253cy) [21] as a secondary set, where the ≥10 threshold is applicable.
Task-saturation risk (new, and the most serious design threat). Published work on this dataset reaches 77.7% (Naive Bayes) to 88.8% accuracy / 93.4% AUC (GLNet) centrally [22], and SHAP analysis ranks suicidal ideation as the single most influential feature [22]. Since suicidal ideation is a core diagnostic symptom rather than an independent predictor, that feature makes the task close to saturated — and a saturated task cannot discriminate between aggregators, which would render RQ3/RQ4 uninformative regardless of the mechanism. Mitigation: run every experiment in two feature regimes — (a) full features, and (b) screening features with the suicidal-ideation item removed — and treat (b) as the headline regime. We will report the centralized ceiling in both regimes before running any FL comparison, so the discriminative headroom is known in advance rather than discovered afterwards.

Backups. ikynahidwin/depression-student-dataset (smaller, cleaner cross-check) and shariful07/student-mental-health (Malaysia cohort, cross-population robustness). Licensing for all sets is Kaggle/Mendeley-public and will be cited explicitly in the final paper.

9.2 Federated setup

Partition records across K = 10 simulated institutions with Dirichlet non-IID sampling over the label (α = 0.3, matching the frozen protocol), reusing dirichlet_partition. Model: a small tabular MLP (2–3 hidden layers) with its final linear layer named classifier so CSSV reads it unchanged. Protocol adapted from the frozen PROTOCOL in run_noise_sweep.py: 10 clients, 5 active/round, ~40 rounds, 5 seeds (up from 3 — the prior study's ±0.13 variance on APTOS showed 3 is too few for a non-inferiority test).

9.3 Adversary model: graded free-riders

Revision 1 specified a single free-rider mode, which risks a trivially easy result. We instead define an attack ladder of increasing subtlety, and report detection and reward capture at each rung:

Rung Free-rider behaviour Expected difficulty
F0 Honest (control) —
F1 Random / Gaussian-noise update trivial to detect
F2 Zero-effort: returns the global model unchanged easy (no update signal)
F3 Trains on a small random subset (10% of local data) moderate
F4 Trains honestly then scales the update by a factor c > 1 to inflate apparent contribution hard — targets the cosine term
F5 Label-noise client (60% flips), i.e. the §5.1 adversary hardest; partially informative

Sweep free-rider fraction ∈ {0%, 20%, 40%} independently of label-noise fraction ∈ {0%, 20%, 40%}. Reporting F1–F5 separately is what makes RQ2 a real test: a mechanism that only defeats F1–F2 is not robust, and we will say so if that is what we find.

9.4 Baselines, metrics, ablations

Baselines. The nine existing presets, plus three incentive controls — no-incentive, equal-split, size-proportional — plus raw-CSSV reward (the ablation that isolates §5.2), all versus FedShapIn and its reputation-gated / reverse-auction variants.

Metrics.

  • Model: balanced accuracy and AUC (headline, given the 58.6% base rate — plain accuracy is misleading here), per-class accuracy, and a majority-class-only reference (0.586) as a sanity floor.
  • Fairness: Jain's index on per-client accuracy and on cumulative payoff.
  • Incentive fidelity (RQ1): Spearman correlation between cumulative reward and a ground-truth contribution, computed by leave-one-out retraining on held-out data. Feasible here: K=10 and a small MLP make 10 LOO retrains cheap, unlike the ResNet setting.
  • Robustness (RQ2): per-rung free-rider detection AUC, share of cumulative budget captured by free-riders (target ≈ 0), and global-accuracy degradation vs clean.
  • Economic: budget-balance residual (should be exactly 0), and per-client utility under a stated cost model for IR.

Ablations. Signal composition (α,β,γ: CSSV-only vs +loss vs +reputation); reward rule (proportional vs reputation-gated vs auction); budget size B; reputation floor; EMA momentum; feature regime (full vs screening).

10. Pre-registered success criteria and falsification

Why this section exists. Revision 1's expected outcome was "competitive/better global accuracy" plus higher reward correlation. The accuracy half re-opened the comparison that §5.1 already lost. These criteria are fixed before any run, and the accuracy criterion is non-inferiority only.

10.1 Primary criteria (the proposal succeeds only if all hold)

# Criterion Threshold Maps to
C1 Reward–contribution fidelity Spearman ρ(reward, LOO value) exceeds each of size-proportional, equal-split, and raw-CSSV by ≥ 0.15, with non-overlapping 95% bootstrap CIs over 5 seeds RQ1
C2 Free-rider starvation Free-riders capture ≤ 5% of cumulative budget at a 40% free-rider fraction (equal-split captures ~40%), for rungs F1–F4 RQ2
C3 Detection quality Free-rider detection AUC ≥ 0.85 on rungs F1–F4 RQ2
C4 Accuracy non-inferiority FedShapIn balanced accuracy ≥ FedDyn − 0.01, one-sided TOST at α=0.05 over 5 seeds RQ3
C5 Budget balance Residual |Σr_k − B| = 0 exactly, every round §8.3

10.2 Falsification conditions (the proposal fails, and we report it)

  • C1 fails → the blended score does not rank clients better than dataset size. This is the sharpest test, because it directly probes whether the §5.2 monotonicity transfers to tabular data. If it fails, the honest conclusion is that the FedPowerShap contribution signal is modality-specific, and the paper becomes a negative result extending §5.1 rather than an incentive mechanism.
  • C2/C3 fail on F1–F2 (the easy rungs) → the mechanism is not viable at all.
  • C2/C3 fail only on F4 (update scaling) → report as a characterised limitation with the specific attack that defeats it; a mechanism robust to F1–F3 but not F4 is still publishable if stated as such.
  • C4 fails → down-weighting costs more accuracy than the incentive layer justifies; report the fairness–accuracy trade-off curve and recommend the reputation-gated variant (which touches reward only, not aggregation weights) as the deployable configuration.

10.3 Commitment on the replication of the prior negative result

We will run the FedDyn vs proposed-aggregation comparison on the tabular data (RQ4) and report it whether or not down-weighting wins. If §5.1 replicates — no aggregation win on a fourth, non-imaging dataset — that strengthens the prior characterization and will be reported as such in the same paper. The incentive results (C1–C3, C5) stand or fall independently of it, because they do not depend on the aggregation weights improving accuracy.

Deliverable: a conference/journal paper with reproducible code, extending the existing FedPowerShap report.

Risks & mitigations.

Risk Severity Mitigation
The §5.2 signal does not transfer to tabular data (the core risk) High Made the primary falsification test (C1, §10.2); measured early in Weeks 3–4 so the project can pivot to the negative-result paper before the full sweep is spent
Task saturation makes all methods tie High Two feature regimes; centralized ceiling measured before the FL sweep; balanced accuracy/AUC as headline (§9.1)
Overlap with FRIMFL [15] weakens novelty Medium Stated openly in §3(c); differentiate on class-specific signal, the measured failure analysis, and the graded adversary — and implement the FRIMFL-style auction as a baseline rather than ignoring it
Ground-truth contribution (LOO) is expensive Low K=10 with a small MLP makes 10 retrains cheap; Owen/Monte-Carlo sampling [8] if K grows
Formal IC is hard to prove in general Medium Scope the theoretical claim to the F1–F5 attack classes; support empirically (RQ2); position general guarantees as future work
Self-selected, non-clinical sample (58.6% prevalence) Medium State explicitly; cross-check on the PHQ-9-scored Mendeley set [21] and the Malaysia cohort
